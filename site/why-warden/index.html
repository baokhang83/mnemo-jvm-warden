Why Warden: how to make a JVM release memory to the OS in Kubernetes

The economics (where the money really comes from), the three workloads where removing replicas is not an option, and the JVM mechanisms that make a running heap shrinkable at all.

The gap: three cases HPA cannot touch

In most production environments, Horizontal Pod Autoscaling is the right answer: scale from ten replicas to two when traffic drops. Warden does not compete with that. It exists for the cases where scaling in is not possible.

  1. Minimum-replica HA baselines. 3 services × 3 replicas × 3 availability zones is 27 pods that cannot scale to zero; the floor is for failover, not load. Worked example from the project docs: nine replicas at 4 GB per JVM reserve 36 GB overnight to serve no requests. Shrinking each replica to roughly 1 GB while keeping all nine alive turns that into about 9 GB. This is an illustration of the arithmetic, not a measurement.
  2. Slow-booting monoliths. A 2 to 5 minute boot makes scale-to-few a liability at the next spike. Warden keeps pods warm (JVM up, classes loaded, pools primed); growing back is a sub-second resize, not a cold start.
  3. Stateful nodes and distributed caches. Killing a replica forces rebalancing and cache loss. Warden shrinks in place: it flushes evictable entries and idle pages, runs a deep GC, uncommits the freed pages, and keeps the hot working set.

Where the money comes from: requests, not limits

Lowering a Pod’s memory limit saves nothing: you still pay for the node. Cost follows requests, because that is what the scheduler bin-packs against and what the Cluster Autoscaler uses to decide a node can be drained.

Warden’s action boundary ends at “lower the request”. It resizes an existing pod in place; it never changes the number of pods (that is HPA) or nodes (that is the autoscaler). On a fixed node pool with no autoscaler, a lower request frees schedulable capacity but zero billed capacity.

The JVM knobs: ZGC vs Shenandoah vs G1 in a container

You cannot change -Xmx on a running JVM. What is movable at runtime is the soft ceiling and the committed memory.

The JVM already does half of this. Warden’s job is the orchestration: drive these knobs from a schedule and coordinate them with a Kubernetes in-place Pod resize, which the JVM cannot do on its own. For the order and the gate, see the architecture.

How small can the limit go?

smallest profile limit
    >= -Xmx
    + non-heap overhead (commonly 100-150 MiB for a Spring Boot service)
    + working-set headroom

Example: -Xmx200m plus about 130 MiB non-heap is about 330 MiB expected RSS, so a 400 MiB off-peak limit leaves about 70 MiB of headroom. Warden does not squeeze an application into a smaller container than it needs; it follows the working set down in quiet hours. Lower the request, not only the limit.

Warden vs VPA in one line

VPA now also resizes in place but is JVM-blind: it observes RSS and cannot move it. VPA resizes the box and hopes the JVM cooperates; Warden makes the JVM cooperate first, then resizes. The full comparison is on the home page.
